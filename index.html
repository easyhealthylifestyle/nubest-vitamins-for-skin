Five vitamins your skin relies on, and where to find them on your plate

Vitamins C, E, A, D and B3 each play a part in how skin is built and maintained. Food is the place to start; supplements can fill gaps. This guide shows which everyday foods provide each one, how much adults need, and where the safe upper limits sit.

The five vitamins

Vitamin C

The body needs vitamin C to make collagen, a structural protein in skin. It also works as an antioxidant. It is water-soluble, so the body doesn’t store much of it.

Food sources: citrus fruits, red bell peppers, kiwi, strawberries, broccoli, tomatoes.

Vitamin E

A fat-soluble antioxidant that helps protect cells from damage by free radicals. It is often paired with vitamin C in skin care products.

Food sources: almonds, sunflower seeds, peanuts, spinach, vegetable oils such as sunflower and safflower oil.

Vitamin A

Needed for normal growth and development of cells throughout the body. Retinoids, compounds related to vitamin A, are widely used in topical skin care, usually at night and introduced slowly.

Food sources: eggs, dairy and fish (preformed vitamin A); carrots, sweet potatoes, spinach and other orange or leafy vegetables (beta-carotene, which the body converts).

Vitamin D

The skin makes vitamin D when exposed to sunlight, but sun exposure also raises skin cancer risk, so sun protection still matters. Food and supplements are other ways to get it.

Food sources: fatty fish such as salmon and tuna, egg yolks, UV-exposed mushrooms, fortified milk and cereals.

Vitamin B3 (niacin)

A B vitamin the body uses to turn food into energy and keep cells working. Niacinamide, one form of B3, is a common ingredient in topical skin care.

Food sources: chicken, turkey, tuna, salmon, peanuts, brown rice, fortified cereals.

Before you add a supplement

  • Add up every source. A multivitamin plus a skin formula can double up on the same vitamins. Compare the totals with the upper limits.
  • Pregnancy needs extra care. High-dose supplements of preformed vitamin A should be avoided by anyone who is or might be pregnant.
  • Mention supplements to your doctor or pharmacist. Some vitamins interact with medicines, including acne and psoriasis medicines made from vitamin A.
  • For topical products, patch test first. Try a small amount on your inner arm for 24 to 48 hours, add one new product at a time, and wear broad-spectrum sunscreen during the day.
  • Stop and get help for burning that doesn’t ease, hives, blistering, or swelling of the face, lips or tongue.
